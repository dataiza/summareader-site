Privacy policy

Last updated: 14 October 2026 · Applies to the SummaReader app for Linux, Android, iOS and macOS, in its free and its Premium edition, and to these pages.

The short version

We do not collect data about you. The app has no account, no analytics, no crash reporting and no advertising, and we operate no server that receives your library.

Your library is kept on your own devices. When you sync it between your devices, it is encrypted on the device before it leaves, and the sync server cannot read it. We do not operate a sync server; you or a person you trust operates it.

The app must use the internet to get what you follow. This policy is mostly about where the app connects, and what each of those places can see.

Some things leave the device only because you switch them on. Others happen on their own, and this policy names them: the system backup of your phone, the hidden page renderer, and one Google component in the Android app.

The two editions

SummaReader comes in two editions, which are two different apps:

  • Free. A reader: it gets what you follow, you read it, you keep it, and you can summarize one item when you ask.
  • Premium. A separate paid build. It adds, among other things, automatic summaries, search, tags, lists, highlights, site sign-ins and the local servers (REST, MCP and metrics).

The free build does not contain the code for site sign-ins or for the local servers. The sections about them apply only to Premium.

What stays on your device

Unless a section below says otherwise, all of this stays on the device:

  • the sources you follow (feeds, sites, YouTube, PeerTube, Mastodon, Nostr), and the articles, videos and posts they bring, with their text and pictures
  • summaries, on-device or from an endpoint you set
  • your highlights, notes, tags, lists, read state and saved items
  • your settings
  • the activity trace (see The trace)
  • these secrets, in the system keychain:
    • any API key for a summary endpoint
    • a Hugging Face token, if you enter one
    • the tokens for the local servers (Premium)
    • the cookies of the sites you sign in to (Premium)
    • the sync keys and the device key

On Linux, if the system keychain (Secret Service) is missing or locked, the app writes these secrets to a file that only your user account can read, and tells you so.

The library itself is not encrypted by the app on the device. It is protected by the device: your login, the disk encryption of the device, and the app's own storage area. Anyone with access to your unlocked device or to an unencrypted copy of its files can read it.

None of this is sent to us.

Fetching is direct

The app connects to publishers itself. There is no proxy between them and you. Publishers see your IP address, as they do when you visit them in a browser. We see nothing. The app's own requests name the program (SummaReader) in their User-Agent header.

The hidden page renderer

Some pages give no text until their scripts run. For those pages only, the app opens the page once in the system's own web engine, without showing it, and reads the text from the result. This happens automatically, after the normal fetch found no article. On Android and iOS it happens only while the app is open or the phone is charging.

When it happens, the page's scripts run, including advertising and tracking scripts, as in a browser. They can see your IP address and your device's web engine, and they can send what they find to the hosts they use.

After each page, success or failure, the app clears the web engine's cookies, stored data and cache. A site that you did not sign in to keeps nothing from one page to the next. On Linux the renderer is a separate process that uses a new, empty temporary folder for each page. The renderer never loads addresses on your local network.

Site sign-ins (Premium)

In Premium you can sign in to a site, for example a newspaper you subscribe to, or YouTube with your Google account. The app then keeps that site's cookies:

  • in the system keychain, filed under an account name that you choose
  • never in sync: the account name syncs, the cookies do not. Each device signs in for itself
  • in a library backup file only if you ask for it there, sealed with a passphrase that you choose (see Backups)

The cookies are sent to that site when a source that uses the account is fetched, and they are put into the hidden page renderer for that one page. The app keeps a session cookie until the site's own expiry date, or until you remove the sign-in at Configuration › Advanced › Accounts. The app does not end a session when it closes.

YouTube access is unofficial. The app reads YouTube's public pages and the same internal interface that YouTube's own web page uses. It does not use an official YouTube interface or key. With a signed-in Google account, YouTube sees that account when the app lists your subscriptions or your playlists (Watch Later included), or fetches as it. The app only reads these lists. It never changes your subscriptions or playlists.

Summaries

On your device, nothing leaves. The app can run a model on the device: a downloaded model (through llama.cpp, LiteRT-LM, or MediaPipe on Android and iOS), or the system model on iOS 26 and macOS 26 that Apple Intelligence provides.

An endpoint you set gets the item. If you point the app at a provider (the presets are OpenAI, OpenRouter, Anthropic and Ollama Cloud) or at your own server, the app sends the item's title, description and text there, and the names of your tags when it asks the model for tags. That provider's terms and privacy policy apply. With automatic summaries (Premium) switched on, this happens without a press, each time new items arrive.

When no model and no endpoint is set, the app summarizes nothing.

Models from Hugging Face

The model search, the model information and the downloads come from huggingface.co and the download hosts it sends the app to. A Hugging Face token is sent only to Hugging Face, and only if you enter one (for gated models).

The update check (Linux)

The free Linux AppImage can check GitHub for a newer release. It checks only when you press Check for updates at Configuration › About. Premium and the other platforms update through their stores and do not use this.

Finding a sync server on your network

When you press the button that looks for a sync server, the app listens on your local network for a server that announces itself (mDNS, the service _summareader-sync._tcp). Nothing leaves your local network. iOS and macOS ask your permission for this the first time.

Third-party components

The Android app includes a Google component that can send data. MediaPipe, the library that one of the on-device engines uses on Android, includes Google's data-transport component (com.google.android.datatransport) and the Firebase encoders. These are the parts that Google's libraries use to send usage information to Google. They are in every Android build, in both editions, whether or not you use that engine. We have not yet confirmed what, if anything, they send. When we know, this section will say so.

The pairing scanner uses no Google library. It is built on zxing-cpp on Android and iOS, and on the system camera on macOS.

Other than this, the app contains no analytics, advertising, crash-reporting or tracking component.

What we collect

We collect no analytics, no usage statistics, no crash reports, no device identifiers and no advertising identifiers. See Third-party components for the one component that we have not yet cleared.

Permissions

Android asks for or declares:

  • internet, and the network state (to know if the device is online)
  • notifications, if you enable them
  • the camera, to scan a pairing code (the camera is optional; a device without one pairs by code)
  • a foreground service (data sync, and short tasks), a wake lock, and starting after the device boots, so that scheduled fetches and summaries can continue with the screen off
  • vibrate, for notifications

iOS asks for the camera (pairing code) and the local network (to find a sync server), and uses background refresh. It also shows a photo-library text, because the scanner library can read codes from pictures; the app never opens your photos.

macOS asks for the camera (pairing code) and the local network. The app runs in the macOS sandbox, with network access, a local server permission for Premium's local servers, and access only to files that you choose.

Linux has no permission system for apps; the app runs with your user's rights.

The camera

The camera is used for one thing only: to scan the pairing code that your other device shows, when you add a device to sync. The scanner reads one code and closes. No picture is stored or sent. You can type the pairing code instead.

Sync

Sync is off until you turn it on. There is no default server. It works only between your own devices.

We operate no sync service. The only sync server the app connects to is one that you or a person you trust operates. The server software is published separately, under AGPL-3.0.

A sync server cannot read your library. Everything is encrypted on your device before it is sent, with a key that only your devices have. The key is never sent to the server. This is a property of the design: it holds even if the server is hostile.

What syncs: your sources and their groups, items, what you read and saved, summaries, highlights, article text and title pictures, and the names of your sign-in accounts. Site cookies, API keys and tokens do not sync.

What a sync server holds and sees:

  • your encrypted data, which it cannot read
  • one token per device, so that it can tell your devices apart and you can revoke one
  • a label for each device, in clear text: by default your Android device model or your computer's host name. You can change it
  • a list of account events, in clear text: a device was added, a device used the recovery code, the recovery code was replaced, the library was wiped. Each event has the device, the device label, which device did it, and the time
  • the last time each device synced
  • your IP address, as any web server sees it. The server uses it to limit repeated attempts, and its request log can keep it. The operator of the server decides how long
  • the time and size of each sync

If you lose your recovery code, nobody can recover your data, not you and not us.

On iOS, the sync key goes into the device backup. The key is in the iOS keychain in a form that is included in an encrypted device backup (iCloud or computer), so that a restored phone can read its library. Whoever can open that backup can get the key.

Backups

The system backup of your phone

On Android and iOS, the system backup (Google's backup on Android, iCloud or a computer backup on iOS) copies the library by default: your reading history, highlights, notes and the activity trace. A new phone then gets your library back. The library is not encrypted by the app inside that backup; the backup service's own protection applies.

To keep the library out of the system backup, switch off Configuration › Advanced › Your data › System backup › Include the library in the system backup. On Android the change takes effect at the next start of the app.

These are never in the system backup, whatever the switch says:

  • the key store's files (the keychain values themselves follow the system's rules; see the iOS sync key above)
  • the models in the app's model folder

One exception: models that the Gemma engine (flutter_gemma) downloads are kept in the app's documents folder, and the system backup can still copy them. A backup that large can also make Android's cloud backup fail for the whole app.

On macOS, a computer backup (such as Time Machine) copies the app's folder, the library included; the model folder is excluded. On Linux the app makes no backup arrangement; your own backup tool decides.

The library backup file

At Configuration › Advanced › Your data › Backup and restore you can save the whole library to one file. It is a plain copy of the library database: your reading history, highlights, summaries and the activity trace, not encrypted. It holds no secrets, unless you choose to include the sign-ins and tokens; then they are sealed with a passphrase that you type, and a restore asks for it. Where you keep that file is your choice.

The trace

The app keeps an activity trace, a record of what it did, so that you can see why an article was not fetched or summarized:

  • always on, kept in the library database
  • limited to about 4 MB; the oldest lines go first
  • it holds article titles, addresses, what the app did, and error text; at the level DEBUG, chosen by you, it also records the decisions inside each job, in memory only
  • it is in the system backup and in the library backup file, like the rest of the library

You see it at Configuration › Advanced › Debug › Trace. From there you can Copy it, Copy without titles (titles and addresses replaced), save it to a file, or Clear it. Erase everything clears it too. In Premium, an agent can read it over MCP only with a token that has the read_trace tool, which must be ticked on its own. The trace leaves the device only when you copy, save or share it, or through that token.

The local servers (Premium)

Premium can serve three local servers, each with its own switch and port:

  • REST (port 8765): a browser extension or a script puts a link or a passage into your library
  • MCP (port 8766): an agent on your machine can search and read your library and, if allowed, change it
  • metrics (port 8767): counters for a dashboard

Each is off by default, and nothing listens until you switch it on. Each needs a token. By default each listens only on this machine; you can set it to listen on your network, at Configuration › Advanced › API & MCP.

They use plain HTTP unless you switch on TLS, at Configuration › Advanced › API & MCP › TLS › Use TLS (HTTPS). Without TLS, anyone on the same network can read a token, and what it opens, when the server listens on the network. With TLS, the app makes its own certificate and shows its fingerprint so that a client can check it.

An MCP token can read the whole library. Some tools are never in a preset and must be ticked on their own: removing things, set_endpoint and set_api_key (which can send your article text to a different provider), and read_trace. The MCP settings tool never returns your API key, Hugging Face token, sync token or endpoint address.

Nothing from these servers reaches us.

The browser extension

The SummaReader browser extension sends the address and title of the page you are on, and any text you selected, to the app's REST server, only when you press its button. Unless the app uses TLS, it sends them over plain HTTP. The extension keeps the server address and the token in the browser's sync storage, so your browser can copy them to your other browsers through your browser account.

Background work

The app fetches and summarizes on a schedule that you set at Configuration › Basic › Background work and Configuration › Basic › Fetching. On Android it uses a foreground service with a notification, and restarts its schedule after the device boots. On iOS it uses the system's background tasks. Background work connects to the same places as the table above, and the hidden page renderer does not run in the background.

Notifications

Notifications are made on the device, from the decrypted library. No push service is used.

How long things are kept

On your device, by default:

  • article text: 14 days
  • full title pictures: 30 days (a small copy stays)
  • items: 180 days
  • saved, pinned, highlighted items, and items that wait for a summary, are never removed automatically
  • the trace: about 4 MB, oldest lines first

You can change the defaults at Configuration › Advanced › Your data, and set limits for each source.

On a sync server: until you delete it. A wipe removes your synced copy.

In a system backup or a backup file: as long as that backup exists.

Deleting your data

  • Erase everything, at Configuration › Advanced › Development › Erase everything, deletes every source, item, summary and highlight on this device, and the trace. It keeps your settings, your keys, your sync pairing and your site sign-ins.
  • To remove site sign-ins: Configuration › Advanced › Accounts.
  • To remove the synced copy: the wipe in Configuration › Advanced › Sync.
  • Uninstalling the app removes its data from that device. It does not remove copies in a system backup, in a library backup file, or on a sync server. On iOS and macOS, items in the system keychain can stay after the app is removed.

Your rights

Under the GDPR you have the right of access, correction, erasure, restriction, portability and objection. Most of your data is only on your device, so you use most of these rights in the app:

  • Access and portability: the library is on your device. Configuration › Advanced › Your data exports your sources (OPML) and the whole library (backup file).
  • Erasure: see Deleting your data.
  • Anything else: write to development@dataiza.sk.

You also have the right to complain to the supervisory authority, the Úrad na ochranu osobných údajov Slovenskej republiky (Office for Personal Data Protection of the Slovak Republic), or to the authority of the EU country where you live. [Contact details of the authority — to be filled.]

Children

The app is not made for children, and it collects nothing from anyone, children included.

These pages

The pages at summareader.dataiza.sk set no cookies, run no analytics, and load nothing from other hosts. The web server records what web servers record to deliver a page.

Apple privacy manifest and store forms

The iOS and macOS apps carry an Apple privacy manifest. It declares no tracking, no tracking domains and no collected data, and one system interface used for a required reason (file timestamps). The Google Play data-safety form and Apple's privacy labels give the same information as this policy.

Changes

The app links this policy at Configuration › About › Privacy policy, next to the end-user licence and the terms. The app does not connect to anything to find this out: the notice comes with the update. The date at the top of this page is the date of the change.
